## Who I am underneath the job titles

I am a Mechanical Engineer by training. That matters more than it sounds.

Engineering school does not teach you technologies - it teaches you to think about systems. How they carry load. Where they fail. What the failure of one component does to everything downstream. I spent four years learning that before I ever touched a network switch, and it changed how I see infrastructure permanently. Most engineers learn to configure. I learned to reason. That gap shows up in every troubleshooting session, every architecture conversation, every moment when something breaks at the worst possible time and someone has to stay calm and think.

After engineering school I did not take the obvious path. I went to Jamaica and ran the entire IT function for a US luxury retail chain operating across cruise ship tourism ports - alone, across multiple sites, with zero tolerance for downtime and no one to call when things went wrong. POS systems. Surveillance networks. Servers. Client-facing digital infrastructure. All of it, one person. That environment forged something that no certification teaches: the instinct to own a problem completely, from first symptom to permanent fix, without waiting for permission or backup.

I came back to India via Isha Foundation's Sadhanapada - a nine-month residential program of rigorous discipline, daily practice, and volunteer work in a global community. People ask about the gap on the CV. There is no gap. There is a year of learning what it actually takes to stay with something hard, without external reward, without anyone watching, until it is done. That skill is precisely what building a new technical specialisation from scratch requires.

Then Pune. Senior Technical Analyst to Team Lead in fifteen months. Enterprise CCTV, VMS, network deployments. A team of ten. SOPs, QA systems, KPIs, cross-functional coordination, and client escalations. I built the team, built the processes, and built a standard of work the team held even when I was not in the room. Then I resigned - not because the work was not good, but because staying would have meant choosing comfort over direction.

## What I am building now, and why

The network is becoming intelligent. Not metaphorically - literally. AI systems are already doing anomaly detection, log correlation, incident triage, and config validation faster and more consistently than human operators. DevOps practices that transformed software delivery are migrating into network operations. Zero Trust is dismantling the perimeter model that enterprise security has relied on for thirty years. Cloud is making the physical location of infrastructure increasingly irrelevant.

Every one of these shifts creates a transition problem - a gap between what exists and what needs to exist. That gap is where the most valuable engineering work happens, and it requires people who understand both sides of it. The old architecture and the new one. The physical infrastructure and the cloud layer above it. The network policy and the security philosophy behind it. The human team and the automated systems working alongside them.

My specific angle: the convergence of physical security infrastructure - cameras, VMS, access control, and surveillance networks - with cloud-native Zero Trust security and AI-driven network operations. These worlds are colliding inside every smart city project, every hospital network refresh, and every enterprise campus upgrade happening right now. Almost nobody stands in both of them simultaneously. I have spent years in the physical security and networking side. I am building the cloud, AI, and automation side now, with full-time focus, and this site documents that work in real time.

## What I actually know how to do

I can troubleshoot. Not as a last resort - as a first instinct. Structured, methodical, hypothesis-driven troubleshooting that isolates variables, follows evidence, and finds the actual root cause rather than the nearest workaround. Seven years of being the person called when something stops working, in environments where being wrong was expensive, built that capability in a way that no classroom can replicate. Permanent fixes over repeated patches. Every time.

I can lead people. A team is a system - it has failure modes, load limits, and tolerance thresholds just like any infrastructure. Building one that performs means recruiting for attitude alongside skill, creating the conditions where problems get surfaced before they become incidents, giving feedback that is honest and specific rather than comfortable and vague, and setting a standard that the team internalises rather than one they follow only when watched. The team I built performed to that standard. That did not happen by accident.

I can talk to clients. During live outages. During failed deployments. During the moments when the honest answer is harder than the one they want to hear. Across cultures, across seniority levels, across the specific anxiety that comes from a business system being down and no one being able to say when it comes back. I know how to give clarity instead of jargon. I know how to hold a client's trust through a bad situation by being straight with them rather than managing them. That skill is rarer in technical roles than it should be.

I can own things. Not manage them - own them. There is a difference. Ownership means the outcome is yours, not just the task. It means you do not stop at the edge of your job description when something needs to happen. It means you track it, follow it, fix it, and make sure it stays fixed. I learned that in Jamaica with no safety net, and I have not forgotten it.

I understand operations at a level that only comes from having run them. The checklists that exist because something once went wrong without them. The documentation that gets written because the person who knew it in their head once left. The runbooks that mean a junior engineer can handle something at 2am without calling anyone. Operational maturity is not a process - it is scar tissue, built from every incident that could have been prevented.
